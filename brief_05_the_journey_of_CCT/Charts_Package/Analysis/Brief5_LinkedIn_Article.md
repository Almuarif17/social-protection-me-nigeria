# Conditional Cash Transfer, 1990 to 2026: An Independent Review of the Instrument, Its Southern Origins, and Nigeria's Record

**The paradigm was not a World Bank invention. It works under four conditions. Nigeria fails three.**

*Brief 5 of the Independent Monitoring and Evaluation Reviews of Nigerian Social Investment Programmes series.*
*By Usman Almuarif Mashood, Independent Policy Researcher. August 2026.*

---

## Why I looked at this

I have been publishing independent M&E reviews of Nigerian social investment programmes for about a year. NASSP-HUP, AGILE, the Home-Grown School Feeding Programme, and N-Power so far. Across all four, one line kept coming up in the public conversation. **NASSP is the World Bank programme.** Sometimes it was said with disapproval, sometimes with pride. Either way, the framing carried the assumption that the Conditional Cash Transfer approach was designed by international agencies and adopted by Nigeria as an external prescription.

That framing bothered me. Not because it is offensive, but because I did not know whether it was true. Before I could say anything defensible about whether the Nigerian programme should be defended, expanded, or dismantled, I needed to know where the design actually came from and what its record was elsewhere.

Six weeks and 26 sources across 16 countries later, this brief is what came out.

## What the brief is

Brief 5 traces the Conditional Cash Transfer paradigm from its origin to its Nigerian implementation. It uses a chronological country-by-country method. Each country's story is told on the same five points: **who introduced the programme, why, for what problem, how it was implemented, and what happened.** The country stories are then aggregated to derive a verdict on whether cash transfer is a sound national response to poverty, and for what specific objectives.

The full brief is 52 pages, 12 sections, 4 charts, 7 tables, and 4 annexes. There is a 17-page executive summary for readers who need the argument fast.

## Finding 1. The paradigm was invented in the Global South

The first Conditional Cash Transfer programme was launched by the Honduran government in 1990. Bangladesh followed in 1994 with a stipend for girls staying in secondary school. Brazil followed in 1995 with two municipal experiments in Brasilia (under Governor Cristovam Buarque) and Campinas (under Mayor Magalhães). None of the three was designed by the World Bank or the IADB. Each was a domestic response to a domestic problem.

Mexico's Progresa in 1997, the programme that is usually credited as the founding CCT, came seven years after Honduras PRAF and three years after Bangladesh FSSSP. What Progresa added was a randomized evaluation design, which gave the paradigm the credible evidence that spread it globally. The Bank and the IADB codified, financed, and diffused the model. They did not invent it.

This matters because the debate about whether NASSP-HUP is an imported programme rests on a factual claim that turns out to be wrong. The instrument's origin is Southern. The financing architecture and the technical assistance that brought it to Nigeria are international. Both statements are true. They describe different things.

## Finding 2. The instrument works, under four conditions

Across 201 rigorous studies pooled by the ODI evidence review (Bastagli et al., 2016), cash transfers reliably produce five documented returns:

- **Consumption rises** in about 71 per cent of studies.
- **Food expenditure rises** in about 74 per cent.
- **Monetary poverty measures decline** in approximately two-thirds.
- **School enrollment rises**, particularly under enforced conditionality.
- **Health service utilization rises**, in both conditional and unconditional designs.

But these returns are not automatic. They appear when four conditions are met:

| # | Condition | Threshold or requirement |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Adequate transfer** | At or above 20 per cent of beneficiary household consumption |
| 2 | **Functional supply-side** | The schools and clinics that conditionality references must exist and be accessible |
| 3 | **Institutional permanence** | The programme must survive political transitions |
| 4 | **Evaluation infrastructure** | The programme must be evaluated with a design capable of isolating programme effect from selection effect |

Where the conditions are met (Mexico Progresa, Brazil Bolsa Familia, Colombia Familias en Acción, Kenya CT-OVC), the instrument produces its predicted effects. Where they are not, the instrument still transfers cash but does not deliver the wider welfare, productive, or protective effects that justify it.

## Finding 3. The instrument travelled. Outcomes did not.

Sixteen comparator programmes across Latin America, Asia, and Africa show three diffusion patterns.

**Confirmation.** Where institutional capacity was sufficient (Colombia, Uruguay), the instrument produced predicted effects.

**Adaptation.** Where capacity was weaker (Ecuador, Bolivia, Indonesia, Philippines), the effective design degraded toward soft-conditional or unconditional. The income effect was preserved. The compliance effect diminished.

**Mortality.** Where domestic political ownership was absent (Nicaragua's Red de Protección Social), the programme died regardless of its technical performance.

A compressed table of the sixteen programmes:

| Country | Programme | Adopted | Fate / lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mexico | Progresa / Prospera | 1997 | Randomized evaluation seeded evidence. Replaced by UCT under AMLO (2019). |
| Brazil | Bolsa Familia | 2003 | Institutional resilience across four administrations. 21 per cent of Gini fall. |
| Colombia | Familias en Acción | 2000 | Confirmation case: adequate capacity produced predicted effects. |
| Nicaragua | Red de Protección Social | 2000 | Closed 2006 with change of government. Political fragility case. |
| Honduras | PRAF (converted CCT) | 1998 | Electoral capture. Programme captured as re-election tool. |
| Ecuador | Bono de Desarrollo Humano | 2003 | Unconditional conversion. Effective design moved toward UCT. |
| Bolivia | Bono Juancito Pinto | 2006 | Universalist alternative adopted because CCT targeting could not function with weak supply. |
| Philippines | Pantawid Pamilya (4Ps) | 2007 | Partial compliance in remote areas. 4.4M households by 2020. |
| Indonesia | PKH | 2007 | Soft compliance where supply-side failed. Strong vaccination evidence. |
| India | Janani Suraksha Yojana | 2005 | Single-condition CCT (institutional delivery). Over 10M beneficiaries per year. |
| Ghana | LEAP | 2008 | Real value eroded to USD 5 to 9 by 2016. Below 20 per cent threshold. |
| Kenya | CT-OVC | 2007 | Officially unconditional. Strong protective effects at 20 per cent of consumption. |
| Malawi | Zomba experiment | 2007 | Definitive CCT vs UCT trial. CCT wins for enrollment, UCT wins for pregnancy protection. |
| South Africa | CSG (UCT) + HPTN 068 (CCT trial) | 1998 / 2011 | Largest unconditional child grant globally. HPTN 068 reduced multidimensional deprivation. |
| Burkina Faso | Nahouri Pilot | 2008 | Marginal-child hypothesis: CCT wins for the children parents would not enroll otherwise. |
| Tanzania | PSSN | 2013 | Weak transient HIV effect; conditionality effectively absent. |

## Finding 4. Nigeria adopted the instrument for the right domains but under the wrong conditions

The paradigm's strongest evidence base sits in two outcome domains: **education access** and **health utilization**. Nigeria's NASSP-HUP conditions on school attendance and health visits. In principle, Nigeria targets the domains where CCT works most.

In practice, three of the four conditions fail:

- **Transfer inadequacy.** ₦5,000 per month, unchanged since 2016. That is roughly 6 to 7 per cent of beneficiary consumption. The evidence threshold is 20 per cent. Deflated by the NBS Food CPI, the 2026 real value sits below USD 4. Compare this to Kenya's CT-OVC (USD 20 per month, maintained through indexation) and Brazil's Bolsa Familia (adjusted upward in 2023 under Lula's restructuring).

- **Supply-side gaps.** 42 per cent of primary health centres in NASSP target areas lack a physician. Pupil-teacher ratios exceed 40:1 in multiple northern states. The conditionality mandates attendance at facilities that in many geographies do not exist or are non-functional.

- **Evaluation absence.** The impact evaluation for the 2016 to 2022 NASSP was not completed at project close. The 2024 IEG Independent Evaluation Group review recorded preliminary results but noted the final impact evaluation report was not available. Nigeria has no independent measure of what its own CCT achieved on its stated outcomes.

Institutional permanence, the fourth condition, remains vulnerable. The National Social Investment Programmes suite was suspended by the Federal Ministry of Humanitarian Affairs in January 2024. The suspension was announced as a six-week review. It lasted approximately 12.5 months. The presidential panel's findings were not published.

The results against target:

| Indicator | Target | Achieved | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beneficiaries in bottom two consumption quintiles | 80% | 53% | Failed |
| Households receiving conditional (co-responsibility) top-up | 80% | 6% | Failed |
| Female transfer recipients | 85% | 94% | Exceeded |
| Electronic payments | 85% | 100% | Exceeded |
| Impact evaluation completed | Yes | No (preliminary only) | Failed |

## Finding 5. The verdict

Cash transfer is a sound national response to poverty for the specific objectives of consumption smoothing, monetary poverty reduction, and increased use of health and education services, provided the four conditions are met. It is not a sound national response for learning quality improvement, child growth trajectory change, sustained employment creation, or structural poverty reduction. These require complementary interventions that sit outside the cash transfer design.

The instrument is worth keeping. **The conditions of its deployment must change.**

## Seven recommendations

Each recommendation names the design parameter, the required action, and the evidence basis.

**R1. Raise the transfer and index it.** Move from ₦5,000 per month (6 to 7 per cent of consumption) to ₦15,000 to ₦20,000 per month (15 to 20 per cent of consumption). Index to the NBS Food CPI on a semi-annual basis. Evidence: Bastagli et al. 2016 identify the 20 per cent threshold. Kenya CT-OVC maintains real value through this mechanism. Nigeria's real-value collapse from USD 12.17 to below USD 4 is a direct consequence of its absence.

**R2. Make supply-side readiness a precondition for conditionality.** Annual audit at LGA level. Where the school or clinic is unavailable or non-functional, the transfer in that geography converts to unconditional. Evidence: Indonesia's PKH softened compliance in underserved districts. Bolivia's Bono Juancito Pinto adopted a universalist design because CCT could not function with weak supply. Both cases show the instrument adapting rather than failing.

**R3. Blend the design by beneficiary age.** Hard conditionality for ages 6 to 14 (where enrollment effects are largest and adverse compositional effects are lowest). Soft conditionality for ages 15 to 17. Unconditional for ages 18 and above. Evidence: Baird, McIntosh, and Özler (2011) in Malawi Zomba; Akresh, de Walque, and Kazianga (2013) in Burkina Faso.

**R4. Anchor the programme in legislation.** Statutory autonomous agency, established by an Act of the National Assembly, with legislated funding, protected mandate, and independent board. Evidence: Brazil's Bolsa Familia survived four administrations through a federal-to-municipal delivery architecture that neither level can unilaterally halt. Nicaragua's RPS died when the domestic constituency evaporated.

**R5. Design the evaluation from inception.** Prospective regression discontinuity or randomized assignment embedded from first disbursement, with pre-registered analysis plan and an independent evaluation partner. Evidence: Progresa's randomized rollout in 1998 is the precedent. Without a counterfactual, the programme cannot demonstrate its effect.

**R6. Fix targeting accuracy.** Recalibrate the PMT formula using the 2018/19 NLSS data. Integrate the NSR with the NIN database and the CBN financial-inclusion registry for cross-registry de-duplication. Evidence: The IEG (2024) review documents targeting as the project's most significant design shortfall. Both reforms are low-cost and high-return.

**R7. Protect the delivery system from political interference.** Fully digitised grievance redress with automatic escalation to legislative oversight after 30 days. Quarterly publication of third-party monitoring reports regardless of ministerial consent. Straight-through disbursement processing to NUBAN accounts, removing ministerial discretion to halt payments. Evidence: The NASSP-SU PAD commits to all three but they remain design commitments. The January 2024 suspension experience shows why they must become operational procedures.

## Four commitments that remain unimplemented

Follow-up reporting for any subsequent brief in this series. Each of these was made by the World Bank or by NASSCO between 2016 and 2024 and remains open as of writing.

1. **The NASSP impact evaluation** (committed in the 2016 PAD, reaffirmed under NASSP-SU) has not been completed. Preliminary results at ICR stage only.
2. **The PMT targeting formula** predates the 2018/19 Nigeria Living Standards Survey. The NASSP-SU PAD commits to recalibration. Operationalization has not been reported.
3. **State-disaggregated beneficiary data** are not publicly disclosed by NASSCO. The absence of disclosure is itself a finding.
4. **The NASSP-SU first disbursement cycle** completion status remains reported as ongoing without completion confirmation in the last publicly available World Bank Implementation Status Report.

## What I want this brief to do

I want it to end one specific misunderstanding. **The Conditional Cash Transfer is not something a foreign agency imposed on Nigeria.** It is an instrument invented by Southern governments, refined by the international evidence machine, and adopted by Nigeria at a moment when Nigeria could plausibly have built the conditions the evidence requires. Nigeria did not build them. That is a Nigerian choice, not a Washington choice.

I also want the brief to give the next generation of Nigerian M&E practitioners a single reference document that puts the international pattern and the Nigerian record on the same desk. The full brief holds that argument, the executive summary carries it in 17 pages, and the reproducible chart pipeline sits alongside so any reader can regenerate the numbers.
