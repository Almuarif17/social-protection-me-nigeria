# Brief 5, The Journey of an Instrument: How Conditional Cash Transfer Design Travelled from Latin America to Nigeria, 1997 to 2026

An independent monitoring and evaluation review of the Conditional Cash Transfer paradigm. Where it came from, how it worked in the countries that adopted it, and what its record in Nigeria means for its future here.

Author: Usman Almuarif Mashood, Independent Policy Researcher. Published August 2026.

---

## What this brief is

The previous four briefs in this series each took one Nigerian federal programme and reviewed it against its stated objectives, its budget disclosures, and its independent evidence base. Brief 5 takes a different frame. Instead of one programme, it takes one instrument, the Conditional Cash Transfer, and traces its journey from origin in Mexico in 1997, through Latin American diffusion, Asian adoption, African adaptation, and into Nigeria's COPE (2007) and NASSP-HUP (2016 to present).

The frame change is deliberate. Four briefs on individual Nigerian programmes established a set of failure patterns in Nigerian social investment. Brief 5 tests whether those failures are Nigerian in origin, or whether they are inherited from an instrument that carries the same design weaknesses wherever it is transplanted without adequate local conditions.

The brief answers one question directly: **Is cash transfer (conditional or unconditional) a sound national response to poverty, and for what specific objectives does it actually work or not?**

The verdict is conditional. Cash transfer is a sound national response for consumption smoothing, monetary poverty reduction, and increased use of health and education services, provided four conditions are met: adequate transfer size (at or above 20 per cent of beneficiary consumption), functional supply-side services, institutional permanence across political transitions, and evaluation infrastructure. Nigeria fails three of four.

The review draws on a curated 26-source evidence matrix. Six foundational sources frame the paradigm and the Nigerian architecture. Eleven country-story sources tell one chronological story per country. Three Nigeria sources cover COPE, NASSP-HUP, and the sibling N-Power programme from Brief 4. Two cross-cutting sources aggregate evidence across countries. Five supporting sources strengthen specific claims. It is a secondary analysis based entirely on public sources. No primary fieldwork was undertaken. Where sources conflict, contradictions are named openly rather than reconciled.

## The argument, in seven paragraphs

**The origin.** The Conditional Cash Transfer was not invented by the World Bank or any international agency. It was invented in the Global South, in three near-simultaneous experiments: Honduras PRAF in 1990, Bangladesh Female Secondary School Stipend Programme in 1994, and two Brazilian municipal experiments in 1995 (Brasilia under Governor Cristovam Buarque, and Campinas under Mayor Magalhães). Each was designed by a domestic political actor responding to a domestic crisis. The template that would later be described as CCT emerged from the convergence of these three independent solutions to three independent problems.

**The moment.** Three concurrent conditions between 1994 and 1997 made the design politically feasible and technically attractive: the post-structural-adjustment fiscal vacuum that had dismantled universal subsidies, the arrival of randomized field-experiment capacity in development economics, and the ideological shift in poverty architecture from universal provision to targeted investment.

**The Mexican consolidation.** Progresa in 1997, designed by Santiago Levy under President Ernesto Zedillo after the peso crisis, was the first CCT to be evaluated through a randomized rollout. Consumption rose 7 to 10 per cent among beneficiaries. Secondary school enrollment rose 12 percentage points. Long-term follow-up (Parker and Vogl, 2018) shows improved adult outcomes for women exposed as children. Learning gains were modest. The programme was renamed twice (Oportunidades, Prospera) and replaced under AMLO in 2019 with an unconditional pension.

**The Brazilian scale-up.** Bolsa Familia in 2003, launched by President Lula, unified four earlier programmes and grew to 13.9 million families, roughly a quarter of the Brazilian population. It survived four administrations, reduced inequality by an estimated 21 per cent of the Gini fall since 2003, and operated with soft conditionality that focused compliance messaging over strict enforcement.

**The diffusion and its lessons.** The instrument travelled through Latin America (Colombia, Nicaragua, Honduras, Ecuador, Chile, Peru, Bolivia, Uruguay, Jamaica), Asia (Philippines, Indonesia, India), and Sub-Saharan Africa (Ghana, Kenya, Malawi, South Africa, Tanzania, Burkina Faso). Three patterns emerge. Where institutional capacity was adequate, the instrument produced its predicted effects. Where capacity was weaker, the instrument mutated toward soft-conditional or unconditional design. Where political ownership was absent, the programme died regardless of its technical performance.

**The Nigerian record.** Nigeria adopted the instrument twice. COPE in 2007 was a small pilot that closed in 2015 without formal evaluation. NASSP-HUP from 2016 scaled to 1.8 million households under a US$500 million World Bank credit, targeting 53 per cent of beneficiaries in the bottom two consumption quintiles against an 80 per cent target, and reaching 6 per cent of households with the co-responsibility (conditional) transfer against an 80 per cent target. The transfer of ₦5,000 monthly was set in 2016 and held nominally constant. Deflated by the NBS Food CPI, its 2026 real value sits below USD 4. The impact evaluation was not completed at project close. The programme was suspended in January 2024 for a period that lasted 12.5 months.

**The verdict, and what should change.** Cash transfer is sound for the specific objectives of consumption smoothing, monetary poverty reduction, and increased use of health and education services, provided that four conditions are met. Nigeria adopted the instrument for the right domains but operates below the threshold on transfer adequacy, supply-side readiness, and evaluation infrastructure, while institutional permanence remains vulnerable to political interruption. The seven recommendations in Section 12 name design changes that would align Nigeria's deployment with the conditions the international evidence requires, without abandoning the instrument itself.

## Seven recommendations

Each recommendation names the design parameter to change, the evidence that justifies the change, the specific action required, and the responsible actor. Full detail is in Section 12 of the brief and in Table 7 (Recommended Architecture).

- **R1.** Raise the transfer to 15 to 20 per cent of beneficiary consumption and index it to the NBS Food CPI on a semi-annual basis.
- **R2.** Make supply-side readiness a precondition for conditionality enforcement. Where the school or clinic is absent, the transfer converts to unconditional in that geography.
- **R3.** Blend the design by age: hard conditionality for ages 6 to 14 (where evidence is strongest), soft for 15 to 17, unconditional for 18 and above.
- **R4.** Anchor the programme in legislation rather than in a ministerial project unit, so that political transitions do not interrupt disbursement.
- **R5.** Design the evaluation from inception, using a prospective regression discontinuity or randomized assignment, rather than deferring the impact evaluation to project close.
- **R6.** Fix targeting accuracy by recalibrating the PMT formula against the 2018/19 NLSS data and integrating the NSR with the NIN and CBN financial-inclusion registries.
- **R7.** Protect the delivery system from political interference through fully digitised grievance redress, scheduled third-party monitoring, and straight-through automated disbursement to NUBAN accounts.

## Methodology, in one paragraph

Secondary analysis of a curated 26-source evidence matrix, following a chronological country-genealogy method with synthesis. Each country's story is told on the same five points: who introduced the programme, why, for what problem, how it was implemented, and what happened. The country stories are then aggregated to derive the verdict. This is an umbrella review, the standard method for answering the target question within scope. No primary fieldwork was conducted. Where sources conflict, contradictions are named rather than resolved. Derived figures (for example the 9 to 18 per cent ghost-beneficiary share carried from Brief 4, and the ₦3,000 to ₦5,000 retained real stipend value) are flagged at the point of occurrence. The brief works within four caps: 40 pages, 40 sources, 4 charts, and 11 sections. The final draft ran to 52 pages and 12 sections after the outcome-domain analysis (Section 10) was promoted from an appendix to a substantive section.
