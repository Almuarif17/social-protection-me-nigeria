# Brief 4, National Power Programme (N-Power), 2016 to 2026

An independent monitoring and evaluation review of Nigeria's flagship graduate employment programme across its full ten-year arc. Origin, performance, and reform options.

Author: Usman Almuarif Mashood, Independent Policy Researcher. Published July 2026.

---

## What this brief is

N-Power was launched on 8 June 2016 under the National Social Investment Office as a consolidation and roughly tenfold scale-up of the 2012 to 2015 SURE-P Graduate Internship Scheme. It became the flagship graduate employment component of Nigeria's National Social Investment Programme, deploying young graduates on a ₦30,000 monthly stipend into support-teaching, community-health, agricultural-extension, and similar public-service roles. Three enrolment batches were onboarded between 2016 and 2020. The programme was suspended in January 2024 alongside the rest of the NSIP suite and relaunched under the Renewed Hope framing in 2025.

This brief is a ten-year retrospective. It asks whether N-Power did what it said it would do, what the record can and cannot verify, and what should change for the next decade.

The review draws on a curated 30-source evidence matrix spanning official government documents, legislative texts, World Bank appraisal material, peer-reviewed academic studies, investigative journalism, anti-corruption findings, and civil society reporting. It is a secondary analysis based entirely on public sources. No primary fieldwork was undertaken. Where sources conflict, contradictions are named openly rather than reconciled.

## The five findings, in one paragraph each

**Finding 1, Origin.** N-Power was launched at speed in a recession, consolidating SURE-P at roughly ten times the scale, in response to an APC manifesto commitment. The parallel US$500 million World Bank credit did not fund N-Power; the programme sat in the government's own contribution and therefore never inherited the donor-built verification architecture, output-based disbursement, published scorecard, or impact evaluation plan the cash-transfer side of the same portfolio had.

**Finding 2, Purpose then and now.** The 2016 purpose was a two-year, ₦30,000-per-month public-service deployment for 500,000 young Nigerians. The 2025 Renewed Hope framing renames the programme, halves the duration to one year, and circulates a five-million-beneficiary target with no costed framework. The instrument is the same. The problem it was designed for has changed.

**Finding 3, Performance.** Enrolment claims across Batches A and B are broadly consistent across official and journalistic sources, but the payroll was found contaminated by up to 90,000 documented ghost names, roughly 9 to 18 per cent of claimed reach depending on the denominator used. The only published impact study is a cross-sectional survey of 99 Lagos beneficiaries that explains 14.5 per cent of variance in the measured outcome. No national tracer study has ever been conducted.

**Finding 4, Stipend erosion.** The ₦30,000 monthly stipend was set in 2016 and held nominally constant through 2026 with no indexation mechanism. Deflated by the NBS Food CPI, the 2016 stipend retains roughly ₦3,000 to ₦5,000 of its original food-purchasing power in 2026. The companion World Bank-financed design contained an automatic CPI adjustment principle as binding doctrine. The domestically financed N-Power stipend omitted it.

**Finding 5, The reform question.** The January 2024 suspension, promised for six weeks, lasted roughly twelve and a half months. Over ₦30 billion recovered from the GEEP and GVG streams was traced and frozen but never remitted to the Treasury Single Account. The Renewed Hope reset renamed the programme, silently halved its duration, kept the unindexed stipend, and added a verification gate on old arrears. The absent core, a published results framework, an indexation mechanism, and a post-exit tracer, is exactly what would make the reset structural.

## Ten recommendations, two preconditions

Each recommendation names an actor, an instrument, and a timeframe. Full detail is in Section 8 of the brief and in Table 7 (Recommendations at a Glance).

Two structural preconditions apply before the recommendations can be operationalised: a biometric NSR-to-NIMC identity link, and remittance of the recovered ₦30 billion to the Treasury Single Account.

The ten recommendations cluster into three groups: programme design (indexation, tracer, results framework, sub-scheme reform), data and reporting (public dashboard, independent verification budget line, outcome-inclusive reporting, FOIA compliance ledger), and citizen voice (beneficiary feedback mechanism, ex-beneficiary voice archive, pre-enrolment public consultation).

## Methodology, in one paragraph

Secondary analysis of a curated 30-source evidence matrix. Eight source categories: official government documents, legislative and statutory texts, donor and international-organisation material, academic and impact-evaluation literature, investigative and mainstream journalism, civil-society and anti-corruption records, macro data (NBS, World Bank WDI), and reformer-side commentary. Every figure in the brief traces to a named source. Contradictions between sources are named in Section 5.5 and Table 6 rather than resolved. Stipend erosion uses NBS Food CPI annual averages as the deflator, with a re-basing sensitivity band. Currency conversion for World Bank figures uses documented rate assumptions declared in Section 7.3. No primary fieldwork.
